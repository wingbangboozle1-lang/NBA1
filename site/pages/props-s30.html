The three products compared on the same evidence, with the arithmetic stated and no market price invented: what a hit rate is worth, why a parlay multiplies variance rather than edge, and what the measured same-game correlation does to a combo.

1. Single bet

One leg, one variance term. This is the only product where a hit rate can be compared with a stated break-even without also explaining a correlation.

  • What has to be true: our number must be better than the number it is bet against. On this site the opponent is a naive reference model built from the same official ratings, never a book.
  • What the data says: the model beats the reference on margin accuracy (MAE 12.98 against 13.62 over 60 out-of-sample games) and the best single strategy is 23-9 with an exact binomial p of 0.020 - but it fails calibration and stability, so it stays at a quarter unit.
  • Break-even at the stated convention: 0.5238 at -110. That is a convention, not a quote, and the site never labels it otherwise.

2. Cross-game parlay

Legs in different games. The variance multiplies; the edge does not.

  • The arithmetic: two legs each with probability p and independent outcomes have probability p². At a 0.5238 per-leg break-even the two-leg break-even is 0.274 and the stated price convention pays about 2.6 to 1, so the parlay is neither better nor worse than its legs in expectation - it is simply a larger variance on the same edge, which is why a parlay bankroll behaves nothing like a singles bankroll.
  • Why it is not free: the same edge, compounded, needs a much larger sample before it is distinguishable from luck. A 0.02 edge per leg that is 23-9 as singles becomes a coin flip on whether a 30-parlay sample is even informative.

3. Same-game parlay and prop combos

Legs in the same game, which is where the correlation assumption usually goes wrong - and where this site currently cannot estimate the correction at all.

The same-game correlation cannot be estimated from the data this project holds
A draft of this page said the measured same-game correlation is 0.82. That was wrong, and the source file says so in its own words: 0.82 is the correlation for one pair, Jamal Murray and Nikola Jokic, across 3 shared games, and the study's own reading of that row is “not interpretable - n=3”. Every other pair in the study shares 1 game or fewer and carries a null. There is no pooled same-game correlation in this repository, none is published here, and the correction is recorded as flag F53.

What that means in practice is the useful part. A same-game combo's joint probability is not the product of its legs - teammates' production moves together because minutes, pace and game script move together - so multiplying leg probabilities is known to be wrong in direction and unknown in size. This site therefore publishes no same-game joint probability and no rho it cannot estimate. Anyone pricing a same-game combo with this repository's data is guessing, and that guess is labelled here rather than elsewhere.
